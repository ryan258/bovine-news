Hay prices across the county have climbed to their highest point in five years, driven by a drier-than-usual summer, though local dairy prices have so far held steady, according to figures released this week by the regional agriculture office and reacted to, by at least one cow interviewed for this story, with visible personal offense.

## By the Numbers

Market analysts point to three main factors behind the increase:

- Reduced rainfall in June and July limited second-cut yields, a sentence several farmers have now memorized and repeat unprompted at social gatherings.
- Regional demand has increased as neighboring counties report similar shortfalls, leading to what one hay broker called "genuinely unhinged" bidding at last week's feed auction.
- Storage costs have risen alongside fuel prices, and at least one farm has begun keeping its hay reserve under what can only be described as light security.

"We're watching this closely, but at this point dairy producers haven't needed to pass costs on to consumers," said a spokesperson for the regional agriculture office, a line that did not land well with the herd, several of whom felt this implied their milk was, quote, "cheap and therefore bad."

## What Producers Are Saying

Several local farmers told Bovine News they are adjusting feeding schedules to stretch existing hay stores through the fall, while others are exploring supplemental feed options, including, according to one increasingly desperate operation, "a rotating cast of vegetables nobody asked for."

## Looking Ahead

Officials say a wetter forecast for early fall could ease pressure on prices, though they caution it is too early to predict a full return to typical levels, or to talk the herd down from what one farm hand described as "a full-blown hay hoarding mentality that's honestly a little scary to watch."
