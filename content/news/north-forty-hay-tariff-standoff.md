Trade between the north pasture and the North Forty effectively stalled for a second consecutive day after both sides escalated a dispute over alfalfa imports, with each territory now threatening tariffs on the other's hay, hay accessories, and, in language one negotiator called "rhetorically available," hay-adjacent services.

## How We Got Here

The dispute began Monday when the north pasture imposed a 40% tariff on imported alfalfa cubes, arguing the North Forty had been "dumping" below-market cubes across the fence line at dawn, undercutting local growers who were, at that exact moment, eating them.

The North Forty retaliated within hours with a proposed counter-tariff on premium timothy, second-cut, the good stuff, the kind residents describe as "the reason autumn is worth it." A spokesperson for the North Forty grazing board called the original tariffs "an act of economic self-harm performed in public," which both sides agree is the most words ever exchanged across that fence.

## The Border Question

Complicating enforcement is the border itself, a length of fence interrupted at its center by a gate that has been found standing open repeatedly since August 13, under circumstances this newsroom is documenting elsewhere. Economists note you cannot collect a tariff at a gate nobody has agreed to close.

"We have asked that the gate remain shut for the duration of negotiations," said a tariff negotiator. "It keeps being found open. Latched back politely. Almost courteously. We have begun to suspect the gate holds no position on trade policy and simply prefers to be available."

Both delegations continue to meet daily at the fence line, a venue chosen for neutrality and because the salad bar is adjacent, a fact neither side acknowledges influences attendance, though both sessions have run long and ended in chewing.

## What Happens Next

Analysts expect a resolution by week's end, citing historical precedent: the last hay tariff standoff, in 2019, lasted four days until both sides ran low simultaneously and discovered their positions had become identical through the ordinary process of eating them.

Until then, residents are advised that local hay remains plentiful, expensive, and, per this anchor's earlier coverage, a form of hope. The North Forty was unavailable for comment but sent a statement reading, in full, "Fine."
