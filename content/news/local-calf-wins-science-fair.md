A ten-year-old from Creekside Elementary took top honors at this year's regional science fair with a project studying how rotational grazing patterns affect pasture recovery time, a project so methodologically airtight that two of the three judges have since, independently and without telling each other, redone the grazing schedules on their own properties.

## The Project

The winning study tracked grass regrowth across four test plots over eight weeks, comparing continuous grazing to a three-day rotation schedule, using a system of stakes, string, and a level of patience the young researcher's teacher described as "frankly not age-appropriate, in a good way."

"I wanted to know if moving the herd around actually helped the grass, and it turns out it really does," the young researcher told Bovine News at the award ceremony, before adding that she has "some thoughts" about the county's public grazing land that she plans to bring to the next town hall.

## Judges Take Notice

Fair judges praised the project's methodology, noting it went beyond typical grade-level work:

- The study included a control plot with no rotation at all, which by week six had visibly, almost dramatically, given up.
- Data was tracked daily using a simple measuring stake system, logged in a notebook judges described as "more organized than most of our farms."
- The final presentation included a hand-drawn chart comparing all four plots, plus a bonus slide, not required by the rubric, titled "What The Adults Are Doing Wrong."
- One judge reportedly asked to keep a photocopy of the chart "for personal use."

## What's Next

The project now advances to the state science fair next month, where the young researcher says she plans to expand the study to a full growing season, and, if there's time, "maybe look into why the north pasture gate keeps doing that."
