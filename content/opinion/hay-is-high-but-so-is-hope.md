I have anchored the evening broadcast for a long time. I have covered droughts, surpluses, and the Great Salt Lick Scandal, which I am legally advised not to describe as "the most fun the newsroom has ever been." In all those years I have learned there are only two kinds of commodity stories: the kind where people panic, and the kind where people should.

Hay is currently five-year-high. This is, by any professional standard, a story about rainfall and yield tables. But I have watched this community long enough to know what a barn full of carefully rationed bales actually is. It is not panic. It is planning. And planning, done calmly, in enormous quantities, at three separate undisclosed locations, is simply hope with a spreadsheet.

## What the numbers will not tell you

The regional agriculture office will tell you second-cut yields were limited by a dry June. That is true, and we reported it, because facts are the business we are in. But numbers do not capture what I saw Tuesday at the feed auction: bidding described by the broker himself as "genuinely unhinged," conducted in complete silence, with eye contact, by parties who had clearly agreed beforehand that no audible sound would be permitted.

That is not desperation. That is dignity under pressure. You do not see that kind of restraint in counties with cheap hay, and frankly, they would not know what to do with it.

> A full barn is not hoarding. It is a community telling next February, politely, in advance, that it has already thought about February and would thank it to think again.

Some have used strong words about the current storage mentality. One farm hand told our reporter it was "a full-blown hay hoarding situation that's honestly a little scary to watch." I understand the concern. But I would remind him that fear of February is not irrational; it is scheduling.

## The steady hand we need

Dairy prices, for the record, have held completely stable through all of this. I mention this not to gloat, though several members of the herd have interpreted the stability itself as a personal insult, on the theory that steady milk prices imply their output is "cheap and therefore bad."

Nothing could be further from the truth. Steady is the whole point. Steady is what we do here while everything else goes up.

So yes: hay is high. But so is hope. And when the rains come back, as the forecast promises and as they always have before anyone was around to keep records, this county will still be standing, still calm, and still holding more hay than any actuary would call reasonable.

That's the story, and that's the truth.
